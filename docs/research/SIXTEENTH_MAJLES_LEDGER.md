# Sixteenth Majles: constituency, credential, and vote ledger

## Scope and method

This is the member-level companion to
[`PARLIAMENTARY_CONTROL.md`](PARLIAMENTARY_CONTROL.md). It separates five
facts that are often collapsed into the statement that the Sixteenth Majles
had 131 deputies:

1. the 136 authorized constituency places;
2. the return named in the official retrospective roster;
3. whether and when the chamber accepted a credential;
4. later changes in the usability of the seat; and
5. a dated political tendency or vote, where one is actually documented.

Names, Persian spellings, constituencies, vacancies, replacements, and
chamber offices come from the official retrospective roster `SUP-051`,
pp. 1–11. The credential sequence comes from the official proceedings
`SUP-007`. Latin spellings are conservative working transliterations, not
authority-file forms.

`A` means an official roster or proceeding. `B` means a named alignment in
specialist scholarship. `D` means unresolved. An empty political cell is not
an invitation to infer ideology from title, wealth, occupation, region, or a
later vote.

## The denominator

| Layer | Count | Meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Authorized places | 136 | The statutory full chamber. |
| Places with a completed return in the retrospective roster | 131 | Five places had no completed election or no separate member. |
| Named returned people | 131 | Ardeshir Shadlu died before approval and was replaced by Nasrollah Shadlu; the place still contributes one eventual member. |
| Credentials approved in session 2 | 98 | The first large legal intake, not all returned deputies. |
| Additional credentials presently mapped in sessions 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8 | 9 | Two, three, two, one, and one respectively. |
| Later credentials presently mapped in sessions 25, 30, and 69 | 14 | Two Kashan/Natanz members, all eleven Tehran members, and the southern-Armenian member. |
| Remaining returned members whose exact credential session is not yet mapped | 10 | Official membership is established, but the claim-level session locator remains open. |

The five unfilled places were Abadeh; Shahrekord/Bakhtiari; the second Kerman
place, because Baghai was also returned for Tehran and no separate member was
seated; Savojbolagh/Bukri/Mahabad; and Najafabad. These are different legal
stories, not a generic five-seat vacancy.

The later waves are particularly important. Allahyar Saleh belongs to the
National Front eight in the historical synthesis, but his credential passed
only on 18 May. The eleven Tehran credentials—including the other seven
National Front members in that reconstruction—passed only on 1 June.
“Eight National Front deputies” therefore describes a returned political
cohort, not eight simultaneous votes in February or March.

  Nazerzadeh-Kermani, Ali Salar Behzadi, Nasser Sadr, and Mahmoud Mahmoudi.

### First permanent board, 22 February 1950

- president: Reza Hekmat;
- vice-presidents: Abdollah Moazzami and Javad Ganjei;
- secretaries: Khosrow Qashqai, Mohammad-Ali Masoudi, Ahmad Dehqan, Mehdi

- president: Reza Hekmat;
- vice-presidents: Javad Ganjei and Nour al-Din Emami;

  Sheibani.

Nariman left on becoming finance minister; Arab Sheibani replaced him in
October 1951.

## Named opening political groupings

The only presently complete named caucus is the National Front eight:
Mossadegh, Haerizadeh, Makki, Nariman, Shayegan, Azad, Baghai, and Allahyar
Saleh (`MAJ-S2`, pp. 260–67). The same source describes a royalist majority,
around forty independent notables, and a pro-British southern conservative
network, but does not provide a clean 131-name party register. Three
independent examples it names are Abol-Qasem Amini, Khosrow Qashqai, and
Nasser Zolfaqari.

Sepehr Zabih shows why no Tudeh or Marxist parliamentary bloc appears:
following the February 1949 attempt on the Shah, the Tudeh was banned,
repressed, and forced underground while the National Front was being formed
as a loose legal coalition (`MAJ-S15`, pp. 161–75). This is a legal and
organizational fact, not evidence that every unclassified deputy was
anti-communist or royalist.
