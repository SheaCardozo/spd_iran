## MAJ-S13 — Azimi, *The Quest for Democracy in Iran*

### Material reviewed

“Restoration of Parliamentary Politics,” printed pp. 118–53, together with
the chapter notes on printed pp. 463–64 (especially notes 23–46).

### What the chapter can support

- **The institutional problem, pp. 135–39:** Azimi treats 1941–53 as a
  sustained constitutional experiment in which politically forceful but
  fragmented parliaments confronted weak cabinets, a growing royal court, and
  administrative institutions without secure autonomy. His count of twelve
  prime ministers, seventeen cabinets, and twenty-three reshuffles is useful
  evidence for short cabinet horizons, not proof that every cabinet was
  equally powerless.
- **The Sixteenth Majles and National Front, pp. 139–42:** the discussion
  connects court concern about the election, Hazhir's intervention, the palace
  protest, the National Front's formation and eight seats, nationalization,
  and restoration of the parliamentary vote of inclination before
  Mossadegh's appointment.
- **The opposition field, pp. 142–44:** British, court, Tudeh, Fada'iyan,
  clerical, and former-coalition opposition are presented as distinct and
  changing pressures rather than one bloc.
- **The Seventeenth Majles and executive power, pp. 144–50:** the chapter
  covers election suspension at 79 returned seats, Thirty Tir, extraordinary
  powers, British movement toward a coup, bribery of deputies, the referendum,
  the Shah's hesitation, and the constitutional importance of an executive
  without an autonomous bureaucracy or security apparatus.
- **“Reign, not rule,” pp. 150–53:** the March 1953 eight-member committee,
  court–cabinet dispute over royal powers, limited reform program, and the
  coup's constitutional consequences provide the clearest directly reviewed
  basis for modeling control of the armed forces and royal ministries.

The notes identify the evidence behind these passages. Notes 31–32 cite
`FO 371/75466` for court and Hazhir intervention in the Sixteenth-Majles
election; note 40 points to Azimi's earlier “Unseating Mosaddeq” analysis;
notes 41–46 combine participant records, the CIA history, British reporting,
trial editions, and Mossadegh's public statements. These references are leads
to underlying evidence, not permission to invent an exact deputy alignment or
private conversation.

### Interpretive limit

Azimi writes an explicitly pro-democratic, civic-nationalist constitutional
history. That is a productive institutional interpretation, not a neutral
probability model. Use the book as a major synthesis for structures and
constitutional conflict; use proceedings, rosters, correspondence, and dated
records for exact votes, attendance, statements, and procedure.

## SUP-048 — Efimenco, “An Experiment with Civilian Dictatorship”

The complete article, journal pp. 390–406, was reviewed.

### Limited useful material

- p. 393 preserves a compact set of then-current AIOC production, employment,
  wage, payment, and revenue figures, chiefly inherited from Longrigg's 1954
  history. These are bibliographic leads and require replacement or checking
  against company, Iranian, and later scholarly records.
- pp. 393–94 summarize the British and Anglo-American settlement proposals.
  The chronology can help locate contemporary proposals but the explanation
  of failure is judgmental and pre-archival.
- pp. 399–401 identify contemporary constitutional controversies around the
  July 1952 crisis, the War Ministry, extraordinary powers, and officer
  removals.
- pp. 402–04 show how one political scientist in 1955 read Articles 46 and 64
  and the military trial's constitutional arguments.

### Excluded uses

The article begins from a modernization-theory claim that constitutional
government was “superimposed” on a fragmented society and repeatedly treats
crowds, religious politics, and popular nationalism in loaded terms. It
characterizes Mossadegh as a civilian dictator, credits Zahedi's settlement,
and makes causal claims about the monarchy, the Tudeh, American pressure, and
the coup without later archival evidence. It is therefore approved only for:

1. the historiography of early post-coup constitutional interpretation;
2. explicitly attributed contemporary political-science framing; and
3. leads to the older sources in its notes.

It must not supply character judgments, coup causation, Tudeh threat levels,
or uncorroborated economic values.

interview and his memoir requires a textual-dependence check.

## Qashqai audio: quotation-control check

No repository claim currently quotes Mohammad Nasser Qashqai verbatim. One
high-value candidate passage was isolated at **official Harvard tape 2,
00:56:23–00:57:33**. Both discovery texts locate the same remembered episode:
Qashqai says American representatives discussed recognition of a
Shah-appointed prime minister, proposed that he take Zahedi into the tribal
area, and that he refused because of his commitment to and political agreement
with Mossadegh.

The official-audio carrier and a 70-second WAV review clip were decoded from
the preserved Harvard HLS segments without changing them. The two discovery
transcripts agree on the episode's gist but differ in exact words. This
environment does not provide a reliable human auditory attestation, so the
passage is **timecode-verified but not quotation-cleared**. It may be used only
as cautiously attributed testimony about the alleged proposal, not as an exact
quotation or independent proof of American policy. A Persian-speaking human
listener must sign off the wording against tape 2 before quotation.

## SUP-057 — manual economic-table transcription

Three additional tables were keyed from 300-dpi page images and checked
row-by-row:

- December 1951, printed p. 63: selected monthly observations from June 1950
  through November 1951, including the abrupt crude-petroleum index fall from
  295 in June to 56 in July and 5 in August (1937 = 100);
- December 1954, printed p. 108: selected annual observations through 1953,
  preserving visible revisions to earlier values; and
- December 1954, printed p. 109: selected monthly observations from May 1953
  through October 1954 where printed.

The tracked CSVs preserve issue vintage, unit, date basis, printed page,
missing values, and verification state. The vintages must remain separate:
for example, the later sheet revises some earlier trade observations. These
tables measure bank assets, trade, money, prices, and petroleum output—not
household welfare, employment, regional distribution, or a single aggregate
“economy” variable.
